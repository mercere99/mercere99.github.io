About Kalah

The mancala family

Kalah belongs to the mancala family of “count and capture” games: seeds are lifted from a pit and sown one at a time around a board of pits and larger stores. Hundreds of variants are played across Africa, the Middle East, and Asia — Oware (Awari), Bao, Omweso, Congkak, and Sungka among the best known. Board-like rows of cup marks appear at ancient sites, and mancala is often described as among the world's oldest games, though how old is genuinely debated: carved holes are hard to date and harder to prove were used for play.

Kalah itself is modern: it was developed and commercialized in the United States by William Julius Champion Jr. beginning in the 1940s (he patented designs in the 1950s), and it became the mancala most Western players first meet.

The rules used here

Two rows of pits, one store per player. On your turn, lift all seeds from one of your pits and sow counter-clockwise, one per pit, including your own store but skipping your opponent's. Three special rules:

  • Free turn — if the last seed lands in your store, move again (this can chain).
  • Capture — if the last seed lands in an empty pit on your side and the opposite pit holds seeds, both that seed and the opposite pit's seeds go to your store.
  • Game end — the moment either side's pits are all empty, each player sweeps the seeds remaining on their own side into their store. Higher total wins.
Variant note. Under the standard capture rule used here, a last seed landing opposite an empty pit captures nothing. In the “empty capture” variant, that lone seed is banked anyway. The distinction matters below, because the two variants have different solved values.

Kalah has been solved

Because seeds only ever move into stores and never come back out, the number of seeds still in play can never increase — the game cannot cycle, and its positions form a layered, acyclic graph. That structure makes exhaustive analysis tractable.

• Geoffrey Irving, Jeroen Donkers & Jos Uiterwijk, “Solving Kalah,” ICGA Journal 23(3), 2000, pp. 139–147 — solved Kalah for boards up to six pits under the empty-capture rule; their Kalah(6,4) is a first-player win by 10.

• Anders Carstensen, 2011 — extended this to Kalah(6,6) (empty-capture), proving a first-player win.

• Mark Rawlings, 2015 — quantified values for the standard rules played here: Kalah(6,4) is a first-player win by 8 (final score 28–20 with best play on both sides), Kalah(6,5) a win by 10; he also measured the empty-capture Kalah(6,6) value as a win by 2.

• For comparison, Awari — a different mancala with different rules — was proven a draw by John Romein & Henri Bal (Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, 2002).

This app's engine independently reproduces the standard-rules result: its exact search from the 6×4 opening returns +8, and its perfect-vs-perfect self-play test finishes 28–20.

What each level does

Level 1. Uniform random choice among legal moves.

Level 2. Greedy one-ply lookahead: prefers moves that earn a free turn, then the largest capture, breaking ties toward the pit nearest its store.

Level 3. Alpha–beta search with iterative deepening to about 5 plies (small node budget), evaluating positions by store difference only.

Level 4. The same search deepened to about 9 plies with a larger budget, adding on-board material to the evaluation.

Level 5. Alpha–beta with a transposition table, move ordering, and iterative deepening to about 15 plies; the evaluation adds free-turn chances, capture threats, and pit vulnerability.

Level 6. No heuristics: an exact solver. It runs MTD(f) — a sequence of zero-window alpha–beta probes that converge on the true game value — over a transposition table that stores proven value intervals. The table is keyed on pit contents only: since banked seeds never re-enter play, the future of the game is independent of the store counts, so one table entry serves every layer of the game's acyclic state graph. Level 6's moves are game-theoretically optimal, computed live in your browser.

Honesty about time. Level 6 really is exhaustive: the very first move from a fresh 6×4 board is a ~250-million-node proof that can take from tens of seconds up to a couple of minutes depending on your device (a progress readout appears, and you can cancel). Once the table is warm, later exact moves are fast. Smaller boards solve almost instantly. If you cancel an exact search, the app falls back to Level 5 for that one move and says so — it will never label a heuristic move as perfect.

Under the hood

The engine is C++23 compiled to WebAssembly, running in a Web Worker so the page never blocks. The interface animates sowing seed by seed with its own JavaScript rules mirror, then cross-checks every resulting position against the engine, which is authoritative; an illegal move can never reach the engine's state. Undo snapshots positions and restores them through the engine. Keyboard: 1–P choose a pit (1 is farthest from your store), U undo, H hint, N new game, +/− sowing speed.
